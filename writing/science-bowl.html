What Science Bowl taught me about learning

On breadth, predicting questions, and why the on-ramp matters.

I spent a lot of high school playing Science Bowl, the fast, buzzer-based quiz across physics, chemistry, biology, math, and earth science. I finished 2nd nationally among individual competitors and served as president and captain of my team. But the trophies aren't what stuck with me. What stuck was a particular way of learning that I still use every day.

Science Bowl rewards breadth and speed. You can't go deep on everything, so you learn to build a wide, shallow map of a field quickly, and to recognize the shape of a question before you've finished hearing it. Studying for it, I caught myself constantly predicting what would be asked: reading a textbook chapter and thinking, “what's the buzzable fact here?” That changes how you read. You stop passively absorbing and start hunting. It's probably why people say to look at the test before you study: knowing what you're looking for tells you what to read.

That instinct turned out to matter more than any single fact. The breadth gave me footholds everywhere, so when I later got into research I already had a rough map of most of science to hang new ideas on. And the habit of predicting questions is really just the habit of figuring out what's load-bearing, in a paper, in a proof, in a problem. Most of learning is deciding what's worth paying attention to, and Science Bowl was four years of practice at exactly that.

The other half was teaching. As captain, the thing I'm proudest of wasn't winning, it was getting other people in. I built practice platforms and ran sessions that reached a couple thousand students, and watching someone go from “I'm not a science person” to buzzing in correctly taught me that the on-ramp matters as much as the material. That's the thread running through most of the tools I build now: learning sticks when it's a little competitive, a little social, and when someone makes the first step feel possible.

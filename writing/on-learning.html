On learning

I participated in a competition called Science Bowl in high school. I enjoyed it a lot because of the gamification of math and science, and it helped me get a pretty solid breadth of science for a high schooler. Although my intention in learning science back then wasn’t necessarily to contribute to research, that shallow interest in just getting good at Science Bowl ended up helping me in research later on. It also improved the speed at which I learned — when reading textbooks I found myself trying to predict what sorts of questions would show up, which sharpened my focus and let me sift through content quickly. It might be why people recommend looking at the test before reading the material: it guides you on what to read.

So why are textbooks hard to read? A lot of the time, the intent of a textbook writer isn’t to educate or motivate a student — it’s to serve as a reference for the broader community. As a result, writers may care more about the correctness of a proof than its readability, and some proofs may not even be that useful to a reader. Are textbooks still useful? Yes — as context material for LLMs, for sure, and as reference material for humans, absolutely. As pedagogical tools it depends on the book. Books like Griffiths’ E&M, which were meant to be teaching devices, will keep being some of the best things to learn from; Griffiths guides you step by step.

And why does synthesis matter so much? When we learn, answering basic questions that require two facts or so is like creating edges between nodes. Synthesis is essentially traversing the graph of knowledge you’ve built.

Research

I work on compiling quantum algorithms for the machines that will actually run them: modular, fault-tolerant computers built from error-corrected modules linked by entanglement. Compiling well for these machines means understanding three things at once: the architecture's costs, the structure of the error-correcting code, and the algorithms the machine will realistically run. Below are the problems I keep coming back to, and the papers and software they've turned into.

What I'm interested in

Architecture costs & distributed quantum computing

Fault-tolerant machines will likely scale as error-corrected modules linked by entanglement, so a compiler has to reason about operations that cross module boundaries as well as the scheduling, memory, and idle time inside each module. At Argonne I designed and built SeQUeNCe.DQC, a full-stack distributed-QC simulator, and came away convinced that entanglement is a scarce resource with its own lifecycle; a compiler that treats remote gates as ordinary instructions misses most of the real cost.

Error correction & code structure

Which logical operations a code makes cheap depends on its structure. With Prof. Swamit Tannu I'm developing compilation strategies for bivariate bicycle (qLDPC) codes, where only some logical Pauli measurements are native and the best optimizations often come from how the Clifford group acts on Paulis as much as from mapping and routing. Surface codes are where this intuition started (the thinking behind my SurfaceScope visualizer), and with Dr. Sathyawageeswar Subramanian at Oxford I'm extending graph-state verification to more realistic noise models for measurement-based fault tolerance.

Algorithms & workloads

Knowing what a machine will realistically run tells a compiler what to optimize for. With Prof. Krishnan Suresh I've worked on getting real engineering problems onto quantum hardware: QUBO formulations for annealers, and structured state preparation that keeps data-loading from erasing a speedup. That work shaped my sense of where quantum computers will actually be useful, and convinced me the structure of an algorithm is information a compiler can use.

Where I want to take this

I want to build compilers for modular fault-tolerant machines that use all three kinds of information: how to schedule entanglement generation and distillation around logical operations that span modules, whether a code's group structure can lower the cost of those operations, and how a compiler can use what it knows about an algorithm to decide where to spend scarce resources.

Publications & software

SeQUeNCe.DQC: A Full-Stack Simulator for Distributed Quantum Computing

Under review · IEEE ICC

S. Suresh, et al.

A full-stack distributed-QC simulator on Argonne's SeQUeNCe: pluggable circuit partitioners, a network-node controller, and TeleGate/TeleData teleportation over multi-hop entanglement swapping. Reproduces the 71% distributed-Grover success rate from a Nature trapped-ion experiment. First author.

GitHub →

PyEncode: An Open-Source Library for Structured Quantum State Preparation

Open-source library · Python · Qiskit

K. Suresh, S. Suresh

State preparation is a hidden bottleneck that can erase a quantum speedup. PyEncode exploits the structure real scientific vectors have (sinusoids, polynomials, distributions) to prepare the state in poly(m) gates, with no materialization or approximation.

GitHub →

Computing a Sparse Approximate Inverse on Quantum Annealing Machines

Journal of Computing and Information Science in Engineering (JCISE)

S. Suresh, K. Suresh

Quantum-annealing algorithms for sparse approximate inverses, preconditioning linear systems by mapping inequality-constrained optimization to QUBO problems on D-Wave hardware.

Optimal Box Contraction for Solving Linear Systems via Simulated and Quantum Annealing

Engineering Optimization

S. Suresh, K. Suresh

Improved linear-system solving efficiency by 30–40% via Box-Algorithm contraction, tested on 2D-Poisson, thermal, and topology-optimization problems. Presented at WCCM Vancouver 2024.
